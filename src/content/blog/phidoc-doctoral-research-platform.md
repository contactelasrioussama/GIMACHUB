Abdellatif Miraoui, Morocco's former Minister of Higher Education, Scientific Research and Innovation, has cited a figure stark enough to function as an indictment: roughly nine in ten doctoral researchers in the country abandon their thesis before completion. Not fail a defence. Not graduate late. Abandon it.

The reasons rarely come down to intellectual capacity. They are structural: funding that exists somewhere but is never found in time, calls for grants and conferences that circulate through personal networks rather than any channel a researcher can rely on, and years of solitary work with little visibility into who else on the continent is asking the same questions. On 16 September 2026 — the International Day of Science, Technology and Innovation for the South — a Marrakech-based platform called PhiDoc launches with the explicit goal of treating that isolation as an infrastructure failure rather than an individual one.

## A Missed Deadline, Not a Missed Vocation

PhiDoc's origin story is smaller and more specific than the crisis it addresses, which is precisely why it is convincing. Its founder, Oussama Elasri, was a doctoral researcher at Cadi Ayyad University in Marrakech when he missed an Erasmus+ mobility deadline — not for lack of eligibility, but because the call reached him too late and through the wrong channel: word of mouth, forwarded informally, after the window that mattered had already narrowed.

The uncomfortable implication is the one that shaped the platform. If a researcher already embedded in a doctoral programme, inside a department, with supervisors and colleagues around him, can still lose a major funding opportunity to bad information plumbing, then the failure is not a personal one to be solved with more discipline. It is a distribution problem, and distribution problems are the kind that infrastructure — not willpower — is built to solve.

## What the Platform Actually Does

PhiDoc is built around six features, each aimed at a specific point where Moroccan and African doctoral researchers currently lose time, funding or momentum:

- **Thesis phase and milestone tracking**, replacing the single distant deadline of "submit the thesis" with visible, structured checkpoints along the way.
- **Personalised, discipline-filtered feeds** for funding calls and conferences, so a researcher in marine biology is not scrolling past twenty irrelevant humanities grants to find the one that applies to them.
- **A directory of Moroccan and African researchers**, built to make collaboration a matter of searching rather than of who happens to already know whom.
- **An AI research assistant** for methodology design and academic writing, aimed squarely at researchers who do not have a well-resourced supervisory team to lean on.
- **A reciprocity-based participant recruitment system for surveys** — researchers who help recruit for others' studies earn standing to recruit for their own, addressing a practical bottleneck that disproportionately stalls empirical social-science work.
- **A mentorship programme** connecting early-career researchers with professors, explicitly including the scientific diaspora: Moroccan and African academics working abroad whose expertise rarely gets routed back home in any organised way.

None of the six is individually novel. What is being tested is whether bundling them into one place — phase tracking, opportunity discovery, collaboration, writing support, recruitment and mentorship — closes enough of the gap to change the completion numbers Miraoui's figure describes.

## Why Marrakech, Why This Date

The launch location is not incidental. Cadi Ayyad University, where the founding frustration originated, is in Marrakech, and launching there reads as the platform closing its own origin loop rather than starting somewhere more obviously commercial. The date carries similar intent: the International Day of Science, Technology and Innovation for the South exists to spotlight science capacity built in and for the Global South, and a platform addressing a distinctly African and Moroccan structural gap — designed and founded there rather than imported — fits the occasion by more than coincidence.

## Beyond Morocco

PhiDoc's stated plan is to establish itself in Morocco first and then expand across Africa and the MENA region, which tracks with the diagnosis: fragmented funding information, thin cross-border collaboration and doctoral isolation are not uniquely Moroccan problems, they recur across the continent's research systems. The platform is bilingual in French and English from launch, a choice that maps onto the Francophone and Anglophone research communities the expansion is aimed at, and it is live now at [phidoc.org](https://phidoc.org).

## Where This Meets GIMAC

GIMAC's own community sits close to this problem. A meaningful share of the doctoral researchers who submit to and present at GIMAC each year are working from exactly the institutional position PhiDoc is built around — inside African and MENA universities, often without the funding visibility or supervisory density that researchers in better-resourced systems take for granted. The same isolation that produces Morocco's abandonment rate also shapes who manages to find their way to a conference call, apply in time, and finish the thesis behind the paper.

For doctoral researchers in that position, and for the professors and diaspora academics who might have mentoring capacity to offer, PhiDoc is worth a direct look at [phidoc.org](https://phidoc.org).
