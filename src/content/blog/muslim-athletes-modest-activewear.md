In 2016, a Muslim woman competing at the Olympics in a hijab was a news story. By the Paris cycle it was unremarkable enough that coverage focused on the results. That shift happened faster than the sportswear industry adapted to it, and the gap between visible participation and available product is where the modest activewear category was built.

It is now a serious segment of a very large market, and one where the difference between brands that understand athletic performance and brands that understand modest aesthetics is immediately obvious to the customer.

## The Technical Brief Is Genuinely Hard

The most common industry error is treating modest activewear as a coverage problem: longer sleeves, longer hems, looser cut. That produces garments that are modest and athletically unusable.

The actual engineering constraints compound:

**Thermal management.** Additional coverage means additional insulation at exactly the moment an athlete needs heat dissipation. A garment covering arms, legs and head must move moisture and shed heat as effectively as one covering considerably less. This is the central technical problem and it is not solved by fabric weight alone.

**Head covering under motion.** A sports hijab must stay in place through direction changes, contact and inversion, without pressure points under a helmet, goggles or headgear, and without loose ends that create entanglement risk. The engineering here is closer to designing a technical accessory than a fashion garment.

**Drape versus safety.** Loose fabric is the default modest solution and the default athletic hazard: around bicycle chains, gymnastics apparatus, weight equipment and in contact sports. Achieving visual looseness with functional closeness requires cut and construction work rather than more material.

**Wet performance.** Modest swimwear has to remain opaque, non-clinging and low-drag when saturated. These requirements pull against each other, and getting all three is genuinely difficult.

**Regulatory compliance.** Competition rules on uniform, colour matching and permitted equipment vary by federation and have changed repeatedly. A product legal in one competition may be prohibited in another.

Brands that staffed this work with performance engineers have produced products athletes actually compete in. Brands that handed it to fashion teams have produced products that sell to spectators.

## Where the Demand Sits

The customer base is broader than international competition, and the volume is concentrated well below the elite tier:

- **School and university sport**, where participation drops sharply for Muslim girls at the point where uniform requirements conflict with observance, a well-documented pattern in the UK, France and North America
- **Community and recreational fitness**, particularly women-only gym environments, which have expanded significantly across Muslim-majority markets and diaspora communities
- **Swimming**, where modest swimwear addresses one of the sharpest participation barriers and where safety arguments about swimming competence give the category unusual social weight
- **Everyday athleisure**, which is commercially the largest segment by far and where modest cuts have proven to appeal well beyond Muslim consumers

That final point is the commercial insight most often missed. Longer-line tops, higher necklines, full-length leggings and looser fits attract customers who want them for reasons unrelated to religion: modest Christian and Jewish consumers, women uncomfortable with prevailing athleisure exposure norms, older customers, and those managing skin conditions or sun exposure. Product designed for a specific religious requirement has repeatedly found a much broader market, which materially changes the business case.

## The Athlete Marketing Problem

Sponsorship of Muslim women athletes has a characteristic failure mode: the athlete's identity becomes the campaign.

Athletes describe this consistently in interviews. Campaigns built around barrier-breaking and representation, released around Ramadan or a major championship, that make the hijab the subject rather than the sport. The athletes involved are frequently gracious about it in public and considerably less so in private, because the framing positions them as symbols rather than competitors.

What works better is unremarkable by design:

- **Product campaigns where the athlete is competing**, and the modest kit is simply what she wears
- **Long-term contracts** rather than tournament-cycle activations, which read as extractive
- **Design consultation**: athletes who have tested the product in competition know things the design team does not, and the brands that build this into development ship better product
- **Year-round presence** rather than seasonal appearance
- **Genuine grassroots investment** (funding club-level access, kit for school programmes, coaching) which the community evaluates far more seriously than advertising

The reputational asymmetry is severe. A brand that gets this wrong is criticised publicly and at length by exactly the audience it was courting, and the criticism is durable.

## The Competitive Structure

The category has an unusual shape. Specialist brands, many founded by Muslim women who could not buy what they needed, built the market and hold disproportionate credibility. Major global brands entered later with vastly greater technical and distribution resources.

The specialists' advantage is understanding: they know the difference between a hijab that survives a training session and one that survives a photoshoot, because they have worn both. Their constraint is capital and manufacturing scale.

The majors' advantage is obvious. Their constraint is credibility, and it is not easily bought. Several have been criticised for entering after specialists proved the market, and for product that community reviewers judged inferior to smaller competitors.

The equilibrium emerging is roughly what happened in other identity-adjacent categories: majors take volume in mainstream retail, specialists retain the performance and enthusiast segments and command better margins there. Acquisition is the obvious consolidation path, with the usual risk that acquired credibility does not survive integration.

## What the Research Should Ask

This category sits at an intersection of sports marketing, consumer behaviour and religious identity, and the literature is thin relative to its commercial and social significance:

- **Does product availability measurably increase participation?** This is the question with the highest social value. The correlation is asserted constantly and rigorously demonstrated almost never.
- **How do Muslim women athletes actually evaluate sponsorship offers?** Compensation, product quality, campaign framing and community reaction all compete, and the trade-offs are unstudied.
- **How large is the non-Muslim segment, and what drives it?** If it is as significant as retailer anecdote suggests, it reframes the category from niche to mainstream, with implications for how it should be merchandised.
- **What are the measurable performance effects of modest kit?** Thermal load, range of motion and drag are testable. Very little published sports-science work exists, which leaves both athletes and designers relying on intuition.

That last gap is the most straightforwardly fixable, and the most useful. A body of published performance data would give designers something to engineer against and give athletes evidence rather than assurance, which is precisely the kind of applied, interdisciplinary work the field needs more of.
